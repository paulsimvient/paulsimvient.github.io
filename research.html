Central research problem

Understanding AI behavior through experiment

How do we know a consequential choice is supported by evidence — when AI and models remain partly opaque?

Can we validate a decision not by trusting what an AI system tells us about itself, but by designing experiments that reveal what it actually does — and judging what that evidence supports?

Decisions in markets, operations, defense, and policy increasingly depend on large language models and other computational systems. Those systems can explain, predict, recommend, and solve complex problems. A convincing response still tells us surprisingly little about how the model arrived at it — or whether the recommendation should carry authority.

Why does an LLM choose one answer over another? What changes its behavior? Does it apply consistent strategies, or does its apparent reasoning depend on how a question is presented? Until those questions are investigated, “using the model” is not the same as validating a decision.

This research brings together computational social science, agent-based modeling, multi-agent systems, and controlled experimentation to study AI behavior in service of better judgment. The aim is to move beyond asking models to explain themselves. We construct experiments that reveal behavioral patterns, challenge competing explanations, and establish what the evidence actually supports before a claim hardens into action.

Opening the LLM black box — to validate decisions

An LLM accepts a prompt, processes it through a vast network of learned parameters, and produces a response. We know the broad computational architecture, but that does not mean we can readily explain why a particular response occurred — or whether it is fit for a decision.

  • Why does changing one word in a prompt change the conclusion?
  • Does the model apply a consistent strategy across different problems?
  • When it gives an explanation, does that explanation reflect the process that produced the answer?
  • Does its apparent reasoning survive when we change the context?
  • Can we identify conditions under which its behavior becomes unreliable for decision use?

These are questions that can be investigated experimentally. Rather than treating an LLM as a source of authoritative answers, we study it as a system whose behavior emerges under different conditions — then ask what that behavior entitles us to decide.

From observation to validated judgment

We begin with observations, not assumptions about intelligence.

A model produces a response. We develop competing explanations for that behavior, change the experimental conditions, and observe what happens.

Does the behavior persist when we change the wording? Does it survive new scenarios? Can the model adapt when important assumptions change? Can another model reproduce the result?

Each experiment helps distinguish a reliable pattern from a coincidence, an artifact of the prompt, or an explanation that sounds plausible but lacks evidence. Validation is the step that asks whether a result has earned a role in decision-making — under which conditions, with what uncertainty, and with what right to disagree.

Conceptual illustration — not a measured experimental result.

The goal is not to claim that a black-box experiment reveals the exact internal computations. It is to develop increasingly strong, falsifiable accounts of model behavior, and to keep decision authority tied to what those accounts support. We decipher the black box by discovering which explanations survive attempts to disprove them.

A concrete example: Can we trust an LLM’s strategic recommendation?

Suppose an LLM is asked to choose between two economic strategies. It selects Strategy A and provides a persuasive explanation — the kind of output that can feel like a decision already made.

Did it compare the alternatives? Did it rely on superficial cues? Would it make the same choice if the wording changed? Until we know, we have fluency, not validated decision support.

We can design a controlled experiment.

Experiment What we change What we investigate
Baseline Original problem Initial choice and explanation
Paraphrase Wording only Sensitivity to language
Reorder Position of alternatives Order effects
Counterfactual One economic assumption Response to relevant evidence
Transfer Different economic setting Generalization
Repetition Random seed or sampling Behavioral variability

Repeated trials could reveal whether the model follows a stable behavioral rule, changes its answer for irrelevant reasons, or adapts appropriately to new evidence.

Even perfect consistency would not prove the model uses a particular internal reasoning process. It would establish a behavioral finding — the kind of evidence that can support, bound, or withhold a decision recommendation.

Three levels of understanding

Level Research question Method
Behavioral What does the LLM do, and under which conditions? Black-box experiments, repeated trials
Functional What computational strategy best explains its behavior? Competing models, causal interventions, transfer tests
Mechanistic Which internal components produce the behavior? Activation analysis, probing, causal ablation, interpretability methods

Decision validation usually begins at the behavioral and functional levels: what happens, under which conditions, and which accounts survive. CoAgentics is built strongly for those levels. Mechanistic work — with open-weight models and interpretability tools — is a natural extension of the same research program.

CoAgentics: apparatus for validated inquiry

CoAgentics provides the computational environment for these investigations. It connects domain models, competing strategies, controlled experiments, statistical validation, an evidence registry, and a human research workbench.

Researchers can formulate hypotheses, compare model behavior, run counterfactual experiments, examine uncertainty, and preserve findings across investigations. The system separates observed behavior from interpretation. It does not treat an LLM’s self-explanation as proof of its internal reasoning — or as automatic decision authority.

From validated AI behavior to better human decisions

Understanding the black box matters because decisions rest on it.

When experiments uncover reliable patterns, people can learn from them. They can discover new strategies, recognize hidden assumptions, improve their questions, and develop better ways to evaluate AI-generated conclusions before acting.

The models also become subjects of continued investigation as researchers refine experimental conditions and challenge earlier findings.

This is the idea behind Thinking Together: people and computational models participating in a continuing process of observation, experimentation, and discovery — so judgment improves rather than being outsourced.

We validate decisions by testing what AI systems actually do. We learn together by using those discoveries to improve how we investigate, decide, and remain accountable.
